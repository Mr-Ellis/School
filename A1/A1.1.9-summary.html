A1.1.9 – Cloud Service Models

SaaS · PaaS · IaaS — who manages what, and when to use each

Learning Objective

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to describe the three main cloud service models, explain the trade-offs in control and responsibility, and choose the right model for real-world scenarios.

The Big Idea — One Spectrum, Three Models

Cloud computing hides physical infrastructure and offers it as a service. The three core models differ in how much you manage versus how much the provider manages.

SaaS

For end users
  • Ready-to-use software
  • No installation or maintenance
  • Provider handles everything
Low control

PaaS

For developers
  • Development & deployment platform
  • Focus on code, not servers
  • Auto-scaling built in
Medium control

IaaS

For sysadmins
  • Virtual machines & storage
  • Full OS-level control
  • You configure everything
High control
💡 Quick rule of thumb: SaaS = you just use it · PaaS = you build on it · IaaS = you control the foundations.

1. Software as a Service (SaaS)

Fully functional software delivered over the internet. You access it via a browser or app — the provider handles infrastructure, platform, and updates.

Examples: Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Salesforce, Zoom, Dropbox.
Real‑world use A 20‑person marketing agency uses Google Workspace for email, docs, and video calls. No servers, no software installation, no patching — just a monthly subscription.
SaaS in detail

Pros: Cost‑effective, instantly available, automatic updates, accessible from anywhere.

Cons: Requires internet; data security relies on the provider; limited customisation.

2. Platform as a Service (PaaS)

A ready‑made environment for building, testing, and deploying applications. The provider manages servers, OS, runtime, and databases — you write code.

Examples: Heroku, Google App Engine, Microsoft Azure App Services, AWS Elastic Beanstalk.
Real‑world use A startup deploys a Node.js web app on Heroku. They push code, and the platform handles scaling, load balancing, and database provisioning automatically.
PaaS in detail

Pros: Fast development, built‑in scalability, lower operational overhead.

Cons: Risk of vendor lock‑in; less control over the underlying environment.

3. Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS)

Virtualised computing resources — VMs, storage, networks. You control the OS, middleware, runtime, and applications.

Examples: Amazon EC2, Google Compute Engine, Microsoft Azure VMs, DigitalOcean.
Real‑world use A financial firm provisions AWS EC2 instances, installs a custom OS, configures firewalls, and deploys in‑house analytics software to meet compliance rules.
IaaS in detail

Pros: Maximum control, pay‑as‑you‑go, scalable, no upfront hardware costs.

Cons: Requires deep technical knowledge; you handle patching, security, and monitoring.

Elastic pricing — pay only for what you use

Cloud models (especially IaaS & PaaS) often use usage‑based billing: you pay for compute, storage, or bandwidth actually consumed.

Traditional on‑premise: buy fixed hardware → pay even when idle.
Cloud: scale up/down dynamically → pay for 15 minutes of a VM if that’s all you use.

Scenario challenge — pick the right model

Scenario 1

A small business needs email, document editing, and video conferencing for 20 staff. No IT department.

✅ SaaS — Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. Zero infrastructure to manage.

Scenario 2

A startup wants to deploy a Node.js web app quickly without worrying about servers or databases.

✅ PaaS — Heroku or Azure App Service. The platform handles scaling and databases.

Scenario 3

A bank needs full control over its OS, security configuration, and custom software for compliance.

✅ IaaS — AWS EC2 or Azure VMs. Full control from the OS up.
