A2.2 Revision Guide: Network Architecture

This revision guide covers the physical and logical structures of networks: the topologies that connect devices, the servers that provide services, the models that define how devices interact, and the segmentation techniques that keep networks secure and efficient.

1. Network Topologies (A2.2.1)

A topology is the pattern in which nodes are interconnected. It determines how fast frames move, how faults propagate, how many devices you can add, and how much it costs.

FactorStarMeshHybrid
StructureAll devices connect to a single central switch/AP.Nodes have multiple redundant paths to each other.Stars at the edge, mesh in the backbone.
ReliabilitySingle point of failure at the hub.Highest fault tolerance; no central point of failure.Fault-tolerant core; acceptable edge risk.
Transmission speedSized per edge port (1 G / 2.5 G / 10 G).Parallel links boost aggregate bandwidth.Core can be 40 G / 100 G while edges stay modest.
ScalabilityAdd ports or stack another switch.Grows quickly in cables and ports; full mesh impractical beyond small n.Scales by adding leaves to the spine.
Data collisionsNone on switched links.None on wired; possible radio contention in wireless mesh.Same as constituent layers.
CostLowest.Highest.Moderate—spend where it matters.
Practical Applications:
• Star: Homes, classrooms, small offices.
• Mesh: Campus cores, municipal wireless, data-centre fabrics.
• Hybrid: Government campuses, enterprise data centres.

2. Network Servers (A2.2.2 HL)

A server provides a service or resource to clients. It is defined by its software, not its hardware.

Server TypeFunctionWhy It Is Needed
DNSTranslates domain names (e.g., google.com) into IP addresses.Computers need numbers, not names.
DHCPAutomatically assigns IP addresses and network settings to devices.Manual configuration of every device is impossible.
FileCentrally stores and manages shared files.Prevents version confusion; simplifies backups.
MailSends, receives, and stores email (SMTP, IMAP, POP3).Email needs a central hub for storage and delivery.
ProxyActs as an intermediary, filtering and caching requests.Provides security, control, and anonymity.
WebResponds to HTTP/HTTPS requests with HTML, CSS, and images.Every website lives on a web server.
⚠️ DNS vs. DHCP: DHCP gives a device its IP address (when it joins the network). DNS translates a domain name into an IP address (when you browse the web).

3. Networking Models (A2.2.3)

The two fundamental models differ in where the data lives and who has control.

DimensionClient-ServerPeer-to-Peer (P2P)
TopologyStar / tiered hierarchyMesh (full or partial)
ControlCentral authority owns data and enforces policy.No single point of control; rules enforced collectively.
ScalabilityVertical or horizontal scaling.Increases organically as peers join, but discovery may bottleneck.
ReliabilityHigh if servers are redundant; single server is SPOF.High fault-tolerance; but depends on peers staying online.
SecurityEasier to harden—central point for auth and patching.Harder—no perimeter; trust via crypto or reputation.
Typical Use CasesWeb, email, online banking, most SaaS.File sharing, blockchain, some VoIP/gaming.
Hybrid Architectures: Modern systems blend the models. Skype uses client-server relays for reliability but P2P signalling for discovery. Content-Delivery Networks keep masters on servers but push objects to edge peers.

4. Network Segmentation (A2.2.4)

Segmentation splits a large network into smaller, isolated sub-networks. This limits the spread of attacks and reduces broadcast traffic.

Key Concepts:
• VLAN: A logical grouping of devices regardless of physical location.
• DMZ: A buffer zone between a trusted network and the internet.
• Broadcast Domain: The area of a network where all devices receive broadcast traffic. Segmentation reduces its size.
⚠️ Why Segment? If a virus infects one VLAN, it cannot spread to other VLANs. Segmentation also improves performance by reducing the amount of broadcast traffic that devices must process.
