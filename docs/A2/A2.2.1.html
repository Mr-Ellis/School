A2.2.1 – Describe the Functions and Practical Applications of Network Topologies

📌 Learning Objective

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure and function of star, mesh, and hybrid network topologies
  • Describe the practical applications of each topology in real-world settings
  • Compare the topologies using the five IB factors: reliability, transmission speed, scalability, data collisions, and cost
  • Justify the choice of a topology for a given scenario

🎯 IB Command Term: Describe

Describe – “Give a detailed account.”

Naming a topology is not describing it. This lesson builds the skill of giving a detailed account of how the nodes are connected, what happens when a link or node fails, how data moves, and where that topology is used in practice.

🔑 Key Terms

TermDefinition
TopologyThe pattern in which nodes (computers, switches, access points, routers) are interconnected.
NodeAny device connected to the network—a computer, server, switch, access point, or router.
Star topologyEvery device connects to a single central switch or access point. The hub is the single point of failure.
Mesh topologyNodes have multiple redundant paths to one another. Traffic can detour around a failed link.
Hybrid topologyA deliberate mixture of star and mesh—low-cost stars at the edge, redundant mesh in the backbone.
Fault toleranceThe ability of a network to keep running when a link or device fails.
Single point of failureA component whose failure brings down the whole system (e.g., the central switch in a star).
Data collisionTwo devices transmitting on the same link at the same time, corrupting both signals.
ScalabilityHow easily a topology can grow to accommodate more devices or traffic.

💡 The Big Idea: A Topology Is a Design Decision

A network topology is not just a wiring diagram—it is a design decision that determines how fast frames move, how faults propagate, how many devices you can add, and how much it all costs. Three topologies dominate practical LAN design today: star, mesh, and hybrid (a deliberate mixture of the first two).

The one rule you must never break

There is no "best" topology in the abstract. The best topology is the one that matches the reliability, speed, scalability, and budget of the specific environment. A home Wi-Fi network and a government data centre have different requirements, so they use different topologies.

🔌 The Three Topologies

Star

Star Topology

Structure: Every edge device connects to a single central switch or wireless access point.

Question it answers: “What is the simplest, cheapest way to connect many devices?”

Use: Homes, classrooms, small offices—where reliability hinges on a single box, which is acceptable for non-critical sites.

Mesh

Mesh Topology

Structure: Nodes have multiple redundant paths to one another. Links may be wired, fibre, microwave, or wireless mesh.

Question it answers: “How do we guarantee the network stays up no matter what fails?”

Use: Campus cores, municipal wireless, data-centre fabrics—where uptime is critical.

Hybrid

Hybrid Topology

Structure: Combines low-cost simplicity of stars at the edge with the robustness of a mesh in the backbone.

Question it answers: “How do we balance cost and redundancy?”

Use: Government campuses, enterprise data centres, college networks—where edge devices are cheap but the backbone must survive.

⭐ Star Topology in Detail

AspectHow It WorksImpact
StructureEvery edge device connects to a single central switch or wireless access point.All traffic passes through the hub.
ReliabilityFailure of one spoke cable does not cause an outage for others; failure of the central switch is catastrophic.The hub is a single point of failure.
Transmission speedEach link is its own collision-free full-duplex segment (e.g., 1 Gb/s twisted-pair Ethernet).Speed is predictable per device.
ScalabilityLimited by port count on the hub/switch; adding a second aggregation switch scales easily.Easy to add devices up to the port limit.
Data collisionsNone on modern switched Ethernet; MAC scheduling is per-port.No collisions on switched links.
CostOne extra cable per device; inexpensive 8- to 48-port switches keep capital cost low.Lowest of the three topologies.
What the diagram shows

Every device has its own direct link to the central switch. If one PC’s cable is cut, only that PC loses connectivity—the others are unaffected. But if the switch itself fails, the entire network goes down. This is the trade-off: simple and cheap, but fragile at the centre.

🕸️ Mesh Topology in Detail

AspectHow It WorksImpact
StructureNodes have multiple redundant paths to one another; links may be wired, fibre, microwave, or wireless mesh (802.11s).Multiple routes between any two points.
ReliabilityVery high: path diversity lets traffic detour around a failed link or node.No single point of failure.
Transmission speedAggregate throughput increases because traffic can flow across several parallel routes; per-link speed depends on medium.Higher total capacity.
ScalabilityFull mesh scales as n(n−1)/2 links—impractical for large n; partial mesh (e.g., spine-leaf) scales linearly.Full mesh grows exponentially; partial mesh grows linearly.
Data collisionsNone on switched fibre/Ethernet; wireless mesh shares spectrum, so airtime contention may rise.Wired mesh is collision-free; wireless mesh can contend.
CostCabling, optics, and extra switch ports multiply rapidly; justifiable where uptime is critical.Highest of the three topologies.
What the diagram shows

Every router has a direct path to every other router. If the link between A and B fails, traffic can go A → C → D → B instead. Nothing is lost. The cost is that with four nodes you need six links; with eight nodes you need twenty-eight. That is why full mesh is reserved for small, critical backbones.

🔀 Hybrid Topology in Detail

LayerTypical ArrangementRationale
AccessStar (PCs → edge switch; IoT sensors → Wi-Fi AP)Minimises cabling and switch count at the edge.
Distribution / AggregationPartial mesh (two-tier spine-leaf, ring, or dual-star)Provides alternate paths; isolates failures.
Core / Data-centre fabricClos, folded-Clos, or full meshNon-blocking east–west bandwidth for servers.
What the diagram shows

The edge switches connect to PCs in cheap stars, but the edge switches themselves connect to the core in a mesh. If one edge switch fails, only the PCs attached to it lose connectivity—the core and the other edges keep running. This is the balance: spend money where redundancy matters, save money where it does not.
