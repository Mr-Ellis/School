A1.3.3 – Compare Different Approaches to Scheduling

🏢 Operating System Recruitment Challenge

Microsoft is hiring a new CPU scheduler.

Four scheduling teams are competing. Your team must prove your algorithm deserves to run Windows 2030.

Every round earns your team points. The team that builds the strongest case wins the contract.

🎮 Starter — Know Your Command Term 5 min

Open the Command Terms page. Find Compare (Weight 3 — high demand).

📖 Open Command Terms
Today's mission brief: Your team's pitch must compare — not describe. Referring to both your algorithm and a rival's throughout. Your final 4-mark answer is the contract bid.

👥 Expert Groups — Learn Your Algorithm 8 min

Each team studies one scheduling algorithm. Use the mini information sheet and the compare table below. You must produce:

  1. A visual explanation (diagram or Gantt-style timeline)
  2. A slogan
  3. One strength and one weakness
  4. A prediction: where would this be used in the real world?

Team FCFS

  • Runs in arrival order, to completion
  • Non-pre-emptive
  • Simple, minimal overhead
  • Convoy effect: long job blocks short ones

Team Round Robin

  • Fixed time quantum, then rotate
  • Pre-emptive
  • Fair, responsive
  • Quantum too small → high context-switch overhead

Team Priority

  • Highest priority runs first
  • Either variant
  • Critical work jumps the queue
  • Starvation without ageing

Team Multilevel Queue

  • Separate queues by class, own algorithm each
  • Usually non-pre-emptive between queues
  • Clean separation of workloads
  • Rigid; lower queues can starve

🌀 The Twist 5 min

🚨 New Scenario

The class has just concluded Round Robin wins for a general-purpose PC.

Now Microsoft reveals the real target system: a hospital emergency system.

Does your answer still stand?

Discuss in your team. Consider:

  • What if a life-critical process is waiting?
  • What if fairness causes a critical task to be delayed by a background job?
  • What does "predictability" mean here?
Discovery: The best algorithm depends on context. Comparison requires judgement — not just knowledge of the four options.

✍️ Contract Bid — Independent Proof 5 min

Silent, individual. This is your team's contract bid to Microsoft.

"Compare First-Come First-Served and Round Robin scheduling. [4 marks]"

📝 Sentence frame:

"Both ___ and ___ are ___. However, ___ is ___ and runs ___ whereas ___ is ___ and gives ___. The effect of this difference is that ___, while ___. ___ pays for this with ___."

📌 Click to see a model answer

Both FCFS and Round Robin are processor scheduling algorithms that decide the order in which ready processes use the CPU [1].

However, FCFS is non-pre-emptive and runs each process to completion, whereas Round Robin is pre-emptive and gives each a fixed time slice before moving it to the back of the queue [1].

As a result, FCFS can make short processes wait behind a long one (the convoy effect), while Round Robin shares the CPU more evenly so short processes respond sooner [1].

Round Robin pays for this with the overhead of frequent context switching, which FCFS largely avoids [1].

⭐ Extension (if time / homework)

  • Programming: Write a Python program to simulate FCFS and report waiting and turnaround times.
  • Research ageing: How does it fix starvation in priority scheduling? One paragraph.
  • Design challenge: Propose a hybrid scheduling policy for a school computer lab. Justify with three comparative points.
💡 Key Takeaway: "No scheduling approach is best in every way. FCFS is simple but non-pre-emptive and suffers the convoy effect; Round Robin is pre-emptive and responsive but pays in context-switching overhead; Priority serves urgent work first but can starve low-priority processes; Multilevel Queue separates workloads cleanly but can starve lower queues. To compare them is to say which is more responsive, which carries more overhead, which can starve — and to justify your choice for the workload in front of you."
