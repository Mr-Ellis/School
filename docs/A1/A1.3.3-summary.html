A1.3.3 – Compare Different Approaches to Scheduling

📌 Learning Objective

  • Describe the four scheduling algorithms: FCFS, Round Robin, Priority, and Multilevel Queue
  • Compare them across key dimensions — pre-emptive status, response time, fairness, overhead, and starvation risk
  • Justify which algorithm is best for a given workload, using comparative language

🎯 IB Command Term: Compare

Compare – "Give an account of the similarities and differences between two or more items, referring to both throughout."
This is the key skill for this lesson. You must hold the algorithms side by side — say what they have in common and where they diverge. Describing each algorithm in turn without comparing them will not score full marks.

💡 Why Scheduling Matters

When several processes are ready at once, only one can use the processor at a time. The OS must decide which process runs next and for how long. That decision changes:

  • How long users wait for a response
  • How evenly work is shared between programs
  • How much time is lost switching between tasks

THINK FIRST — Before you read on

Scenario: Three programs all want the CPU at the same instant — a long video export, a text editor waiting for keystrokes, and a background backup.

Decide what order you would run them in — and what "fair" should mean. Most disagreements about scheduling are disagreements about that word.

📊 The Four Approaches — Click to Explore

Click any card to reveal how that algorithm works, its main strength, and its main weakness.

1️⃣ First-Come First-Served Click to flip
How it works: Runs processes to completion in the order they arrive.

Non-pre-emptive.

Strength: Simple and predictable — minimal overhead.

Weakness: Convoy effect — one long job at the front holds up every short job behind it.
🔄 Round Robin Click to flip
How it works: Each process gets a fixed time slice (the quantum), then moves to the back of the queue.

Pre-emptive.

Strength: Responsive — no process is left waiting indefinitely.

Weakness: Frequent context switching adds overhead. Quantum too short = thrashing; too long = behaves like FCFS.
⭐ Priority Scheduling Click to flip
How it works: The highest-priority ready process runs first. Priorities can be static or dynamic (adjusted by ageing).

Either pre-emptive or non-pre-emptive.

Strength: Urgent, real-time, and critical work gets served first.

Weakness: Low-priority processes can starve without ageing.
📚 Multilevel Queue Click to flip
How it works: Processes are split into separate queues by type or priority, and each queue has its own algorithm.

Usually non-pre-emptive between queues.

Strength: Clean separation of workloads — interactive work is served quickly.

Weakness: Rigid. Lower queues can starve; more complex to manage.

🔍 Scenario Explorer — Which Algorithm Fits?

For each scenario, click the question to reveal which algorithm is best and why. Remember to compare — reference the alternatives.

🖥️ A university computer lab running many interactive programs
  • Round Robin is best here — many users need frequent, fair access to the CPU.
  • Why not FCFS? FCFS would let one long job block everyone else (the convoy effect).
  • Why not Priority? Priority would be unfair to students running non-urgent work — they could starve.
  • Why not MLQ? Could work, but Round Robin alone is simpler and sufficient when all users have similar needs.
🏥 A hospital emergency system monitoring patient vitals
  • Priority Scheduling is best — life-critical processes must jump the queue.
  • Why not Round Robin? Fairness is the wrong goal here. A critical alarm should not have to wait its turn behind a background log write.
  • Why not FCFS? Same problem — a long routine task could delay a critical alert.
  • Why not MLQ? Could work as a structured version — but Priority with ageing is the standard choice for real-time systems.
📊 A batch system processing overnight payroll reports
  • FCFS works well here — batch jobs run to completion without user interaction, and the convoy effect matters less when total throughput is the goal.
  • Why not Round Robin? Fairness is irrelevant — no user is waiting interactively. Round Robin just adds context-switching overhead.
  • Why not Priority? Jobs are all similar, so priority adds complexity without benefit.
  • Why not MLQ? Overkill — one queue is enough.
📱 A modern smartphone running apps in the background
  • Multilevel Queue — the foreground app needs instant response, background syncs can wait.
  • Why not FCFS? Foreground responsiveness would suffer badly.
  • Why not Round Robin alone? It would treat background and foreground apps equally — the UI would feel sluggish.
  • Why not pure Priority? Possible, but MLQ lets each queue use its own algorithm — Round Robin for interactive, FCFS for background — which matches reality.

🚀 Activity 1 — Expert Groups (8 minutes)

📖 What is this activity?

You will be assigned to one of four expert teams. Your team becomes the class expert on a single scheduling algorithm.

What your team must produce:

  1. A visual explanation — a diagram or a Gantt-style timeline showing how your algorithm works
  2. A slogan — a one-line pitch for your algorithm
  3. One strength and one weakness — be specific
  4. A prediction — where in the real world would this algorithm be used?

How to use the team cards below: each card gives you the basics. Use the compare table above and the flip cards to fill in the detail. You have 8 minutes — assign roles within your team (one person for the visual, one for the slogan, etc.).

Team FCFS

  • Runs in arrival order, to completion
  • Non-pre-emptive
  • Simple, minimal overhead
  • Convoy effect: long job blocks short ones

Team Round Robin

  • Fixed time quantum, then rotate
  • Pre-emptive
  • Fair, responsive
  • Quantum too small → high context-switch overhead

Team Priority

  • Highest priority runs first
  • Either variant
  • Critical work jumps the queue
  • Starvation without ageing

Team Multilevel Queue

  • Separate queues by class, each with its own algorithm
  • Usually non-pre-emptive between queues
  • Clean separation of workloads
  • Rigid; lower queues can starve

🔀 Activity 2 — Jigsaw Teaching (10 minutes)

📖 What is this activity?

You are now going to re-form into new groups — each new group has one expert from each of the four algorithms.

What you do: Each expert gets 90 seconds to teach their algorithm to the rest of the group, then takes questions.

The critical rule: You may not describe your algorithm in isolation. Every explanation must reference at least one other algorithm. Use comparative language like:

  • "Unlike FCFS, Round Robin…"
  • "Whereas FCFS runs to completion…"
  • "Both Priority and Multilevel Queue…"
  • "FCFS has minimal overhead, but Round Robin pays for fairness with…"

Why this matters: the exam command term is Compare. This rule forces you to practise exactly what the exam requires.

As you listen to the other experts, add notes to the compare table above — this becomes your pitch evidence for the next activities.

💻 Activity 3 — Human Scheduling Simulation (10 minutes)

📖 What is this activity?

A role-play to see how each algorithm behaves with real processes.

The setup:

  • The teacher is the CPU — only one process can "run" at a time.
  • Four students are the processes (P1, P2, P3, P4 — see the table below).
  • Everyone else is the scheduler panel — you score the algorithm after each round.

What you do: The teacher runs the four processes through each algorithm in turn. Between rounds, the panel scores the algorithm on four metrics:

  • Fairness — did everyone get a reasonable share?
  • Speed — how quickly did processes complete?
  • Starvation risk — did any process get ignored?
  • Overhead — how much switching happened?

The critical rule: When defending your score, you must reference a rival algorithm. For example: "I gave Round Robin 4/5 for fairness, unlike FCFS, which would score 2/5 because the convoy effect means one long job blocks everything behind it."

🌀 Activity 4 — The Twist (5 minutes)

📖 What is this activity?

The class will conclude that Round Robin wins for a general-purpose PC. Then the scenario changes.

The new scenario: the target system is a hospital emergency system.

What you do: Discuss in your team — does your answer still stand? Consider:

  • What if a life-critical process is waiting?
  • What if fairness causes a critical task to be delayed by a background job?
  • What does "predictability" mean in this context?

The point of this activity: the best algorithm depends on context. Comparison requires judgement — not just knowing the four options.

🚶 Activity 5 — Four Corners Debate (5 minutes)

📖 What is this activity?

Four corners of the room are labelled FCFS · Round Robin · Priority · Multilevel Queue.

What you do: Move to the corner you believe should run the school network.

Rules when you speak:

  1. Justify your choice.
  2. Mention at least one other algorithm.
  3. Explain why yours is better for this context.

✍️ Activity 6 — Contract Bid (5 minutes)

📖 What is this activity?

Your final assessment for the lesson. Silent, individual — not a group task.

Write a full answer to this exam-style question:

"Compare First-Come First-Served and Round Robin scheduling. [4 marks]"

📝 Sentence frame:

"Both ___ and ___ are ___. However, ___ is ___ and runs ___ whereas ___ is ___ and gives ___. The effect of this difference is that ___, while ___. ___ pays for this with ___."

📌 Click to see a model answer

Both FCFS and Round Robin are processor scheduling algorithms that decide the order in which ready processes use the CPU [1].

However, FCFS is non-pre-emptive and runs each process to completion, whereas Round Robin is pre-emptive and gives each a fixed time slice before moving it to the back of the queue [1].

As a result, FCFS can make short processes wait behind a long one (the convoy effect), while Round Robin shares the CPU more evenly so short processes respond sooner [1].

Round Robin pays for this with the overhead of frequent context switching, which FCFS largely avoids [1].

FCFS is simple but non-pre-emptive and suffers the convoy effect; Round Robin is pre-emptive and responsive but pays in context-switching overhead; Priority serves urgent work first but can starve low-priority processes; Multilevel Queue separates workloads cleanly but can starve lower queues.
