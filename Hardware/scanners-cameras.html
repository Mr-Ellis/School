📦 Barcode Readers

Barcodes are one of the most common methods of representing data and have been around for many years. Two common types of barcode system are:

UPC-A

Used in retail and warehousing. Can only represent numeric digits.

Retail Warehousing
Code 128

Used in transport and shipment tracking. Can represent letters and numbers.

Transport Shipment tracking
How Barcodes Work
  • UPC-A uses seven bar elements to form four alternating dark and light bars of varying thicknesses to represent each data item between the guard bars.
  • Left hand codes represent the manufacturer number; right hand codes represent the product number.
  • Guard bars show the start and end of the barcode and also distinguish left from right.
  • Right hand codes are the inverse of left hand codes. Left hand codes all have an odd number of black bars; right hand codes all have an even number.
  • This allows the scanner to work out whether it needs to read the barcode from left to right, or right to left – allowing codes to be read when they are upside down.
UPC-A Barcode Structure || ▌▐▌▐▌▐▌ || ▐▌▐▌▐▌▐ || Left Guard · Manufacturer · Center Guard · Product · Right Guard
Why Use Barcodes?
  • Faster to scan products than enter a price in to a till
  • Previously, many shops would close to carry out stock control. This is no longer necessary
  • Barcodes reveal a number that identifies each product, allowing a shop to determine how many items are sold
  • Prices can be changed without needing to update the price on each product
  • Customers are able to scan their own items, resulting in a reduced need for till operators

📱 QR (Quick Response) Codes

QR codes are 2D barcodes and can be read by smartphones or tablets. They can contain links to websites or information.

  • QR code scanners use cameras that are built into phones or webcams
  • The image is processed to determine the layout of pixels which are used to store the data
  • The 2D arrangement of pixels allows more data to be stored than a bar code
  • Larger squares are located at three corners and used to align the image
  • QR codes can store up to 4296 characters
  • Can store website addresses, email addresses or text
Uses of QR Codes in Society
  • Restaurant coupons and menus
  • Mobile concert tickets
  • Estate agency boards
  • Business cards
  • Tourist information
  • Advertising posters
  • Self service tills
  • Wi-Fi password sharing

🖨️ 2D Scanners

These are used to convert documents or photographs into a digital format. They operate as follows:

  1. A scanner head contains a bright light and lens
  2. The focused image then falls on to a light-sensitive sheet made up of thousands of pixels which record the light intensity or colour falling on each pixel
  3. This information is now stored as an image in the computer's memory

2D scanners are often embedded into other devices including multi-function printers and photocopiers.

🖨️ 3D Scanners

3D scanners use a laser to scan across an object. Two sensors detect the distance from the laser to the object, creating a 'point cloud' which is then processed into a 3D representation of the object.

  • Tomography – a process used in many 3D scanners which involves taking images in very thin slices (often less than 0.1 mm thick)
  • Used in medical fields (MRI, CT scans) and industrial fields (scanning objects to create computer models, detecting imperfections in materials)
  • Used for CAD (Computer-Aided Design) and as the basis behind 3D printer technology
Understanding Tomography

Tomography creates 3D models by taking many thin 2D slices of an object. Below are examples of how this works in different contexts:

MRI Scan
Multiple thin slices of the brain are combined to create a 3D model

3D Object Scanning
Layers of an object are captured to create a digital 3D representation

Industrial Inspection
Detecting internal imperfections in aircraft parts and materials

CT Scan
Cross-sectional images of the body for medical diagnosis

📷 Digital Cameras

Digital cameras focus light onto a CCD sensor (Charge-Coupled Device). The CCD breaks up the image into a grid of pixels.

  1. The shutter opens to let light onto a CCD sensor at the back of the lens
  2. A sensor measures the light intensity of each pixel
  3. Once processed, the information is then stored as an image in the camera's memory

These sensors often contain millions of light sensors to capture the millions of pixels required in an image. A high-resolution camera would need to detect more pixels on the CCD.
